Life Is Smarter Than Us

Consciousness, awareness, and the kind of reason that cannot be measured in numbers

We are accustomed to placing ourselves at the centre. The centre of the world, the centre of reason, the centre of evolution. We invented the word "consciousness" and pinned it to our chest like a medal for superiority. Everything else — plants, animals, water, ants — remained background to us. Extras. Scenery.

And yet that same person prays to gods and washes dishes with water. To the gods he attributes meaning, will, intention. The water is a household utility. This tells us a great deal about how selectively we see the world.

I disagree with this picture. The more carefully I observe, the more clearly I understand: life is smarter than us. Not an individual person, not a genius — but life itself as a whole. With all its mistakes, its peculiarities, its slow forms and fast forms, those we call "intelligent" and those we do not even notice.

Consciousness and Awareness. Light and the Ability to Use It

If you remove the philosophy dictionaries, consciousness is simply "I am". The fact of inner presence. I sense the world, and the world senses me. Not necessarily clearly, not necessarily deeply — but it is light. Where it exists, there is experience.

Consciousness is not a human privilege. It is a property of the living. Different beings simply carry this light at different intensities, operating at different speeds. A plant senses light and darkness; a dog reads an entire library through smell; for an ant, the world is a map of pheromones and tasks. A bee communicates the coordinates of meadows through dance; an octopus solves puzzles; a tree warns its neighbours of pests. All of these are forms of consciousness. We call them "instincts" only because we lack the eyes to see them.

Awareness is not the light itself, but the ability to direct it. Not simply "I am", but "I see what is happening to me". It is the brief pause between reaction and action. Tiny as it may be, it is precisely there that a person becomes free. Consciousness is the screen. Awareness is the ability to remember that it is a screen, and not the whole of reality.

Reason and Intelligence. The Lantern and the One Who Holds It

Humanity is infatuated with intelligence. Tests, grades, IQ, comparisons — everything is measured in numbers. Intelligence is genuinely convenient: it can be measured, trained, accelerated. But intelligence is only a tool. It answers the questions "how?" and "by what means?". It never answers "what for?".

That is the domain of reason. Reason does not calculate — it sees. It connects scattered facts into a coherent understanding. It senses consequences. It can tell itself the truth. It can refuse a wrong decision even when intelligence suggests that decision is profitable.

Intelligence is like a torch. Reason is the one who holds that torch and chooses where to point it. Which is why brilliant intelligence without reason so often makes a person dangerous rather than wise.

Why There Are No "Lower" or "Higher" Forms of Consciousness

People love hierarchies. Who is smarter? Who is higher? Who is more complex? These are all games of the human ego. Remove the crown, and something simple becomes visible: reason arises wherever life exists. Each form simply solves its own tasks with its own instruments. The ant solves ant problems. The bee solves bee problems. The tree solves its own, botanical ones. A fungal network manages an underground system, distributing resources more wisely than many corporations.

We fail to see reason in other creatures only because we look through human eyes. There are no "lower" or "higher" forms of consciousness. There are different ways of being intelligent. Each suited to its task. Each perfect in its own way.

Part Two

Global Reason, the Bank of Experience, and the Purposefulness That Does Not Depend on Man

When you observe life for a long time without trying to explain it, without wanting to fit it into a theory, without the habit of labelling — you begin to notice a pattern: there is too much order in the chaos. Too much meaning in the coincidences. Too much logic in evolution.

And sooner or later a question arises that most people are afraid even to formulate: where does all the experience that life produces go? We are accustomed to thinking that experience disappears along with the organism. But if that were so, evolution would be moving blind. Yet it moves, for all its apparent chaos, more precisely than most human systems.

The Bank of Experience

If you regard life as a single process rather than a collection of separate biological species, something becomes visible: life collects data. Every form — a person, a dog, a tree, a bacterium — gives it a unique piece of experience. Pain is experience. Joy is experience. Mistake is experience. Success is experience. The collective behaviour of ants is experience. The slow growth of an oak is experience. The navigation of birds by magnetic field is experience.

If this experience disappeared, life would begin "from zero" each time. But it does not begin again. It continues. It improves. It optimises. This is why I call it the Bank of Experience — not in a mystical sense, but as an image for understanding a simple fact: nothing in a living system is wasted.

And if water can indeed store and transmit information — and there are many indirect signs that it can — then it is the ideal medium for this Bank. Water existed before us. It will exist after us. And it passes through all living things without distinction.

Purposefulness

Life does not choose its form by aesthetics. It chooses by task. Is organic matter a convenient instrument for accumulating experience? Very well. Speed is needed — animals appear. Durability is needed — trees appear. A collective model is needed — ants and bees. Flexibility is needed — the human being. Everything is governed not by chance but by a task: to increase the diversity and depth of experience.

We are accustomed to seeing evolution as a struggle for survival. But seen more broadly, it is a struggle not for the life of an organism, but for finding new ways of being intelligent. Life does not "happen" — it learns.

Reason as a Property of Life, Not of Man

Man believes he alone possesses reason. This is as amusing as if an ant decided that only it knows how to build tunnels. Reason arises wherever there is the ability to learn, to react, to change behavioural patterns, to act purposefully. Then reason belongs to all: to the tree — in its biochemical decisions; to the ant — in its collective system; to the bee — in its navigational dance; to the fungal network — in its underground logistics; to the human being — in reflection and choice.

Only man has turned reason into a cult, placed it on a pedestal, and declared it the pinnacle of evolution. But if reason is a property of life rather than of man, then we are simply one of the variants of its expression. Not the best. Not the worst. Suited to our particular task.

Part Three

Sapiocracy: Not the Power of the Clever, but a System That Learns

When you observe life as a coherent process for a long time, something becomes clear: no single organism governs the system as a whole. What governs is always the mechanism of selection — experience that becomes consolidated and turns into a norm. Among ants — selection by the efficiency of the colony. Among bees — by the stability of the hive. Among trees — by resilience and adaptation. Everywhere the same principle: not power, but selection. Not control, but purposefulness. Not force, but the stability of the system.

And when you transfer this logic to the human being, a question arises involuntarily: why do we still choose leaders by their words, emotions, fears, interests, and manipulations, rather than by their capacity to understand and foresee?

Sapiocracy Is a Filter, Not Power

The word "power" is superfluous here entirely. Because Sapiocracy is not a political model, but a mechanism for selecting those capable of making decisions that increase the collective experience rather than destroying it. Intelligence gives speed — but speed can drive you into an abyss. Force gives power — but power without reason leads to corruption. Charisma gives influence — but influence without inner honesty breeds a cult of personality. Only reason gives the capacity to see consequences, not just advantage.

Sapiocracy says: the right to make decisions should be granted not for birth, not for money, not for slogans, but for the ability to take account of life in all its forms. That is, for experience — not biographical, but lived, moral, cognitive, emotional.

Why Tests, Not Elections

People often choose leaders not with reason, but with fears, hopes, irritation, grievances. Elections are about the mood of the crowd, not the capacity to govern. Nature's system does not delegate governance to millions of organisms. It delegates functions to those capable of performing them: the queen bee — because she has been tested and is suitable; the wolf pack leader — because he is the best strategist, not the best orator; the fungal network — because it operates as a network.

In Sapiocracy this role is played by testing. Not in the sense of an examination, but in the sense of verifying the stability of thinking, honesty, the capacity for empathy, and an understanding of the complexity of systems. Reason cannot be measured in numbers, but the capacity of a person not to destroy the system can be verified.

Sapiocracy Is a Continuation of Life's Logic

If life collects the experience of all forms of existence, then human society is part of that system. Every human decision adds something to the collective Bank of Experience: the destruction of forests is bad experience; the restoration of ecosystems is good; corruption is a dead end; transparency is developmental. Seen this way, Sapiocracy becomes not a utopia, but a natural stage in the development of human communities. It is an attempt to bring governance to the level at which trees, ants, bees, and rivers operate — the level of balance, not struggle.

It does not require a person to be a saint or extraordinarily intelligent. It requires only one thing: that a person knows their place within the system of life. Not above. Not below. In the current.

Part Four

Ecomidas: When the Economy Returns to Its Original Function — to Sustain Life

If Sapiocracy is an attempt to align human governance with the logic of life, then Ecomidas is an attempt to align the economy with that same logic. The economy, like governance, has long since moved beyond the control of reason. It works not for life, but for the short-term interests of people who often do not even understand which systems sustain their existence.

We destroy forests faster than we can understand that a forest is not a tree, but a mechanism for regulating climate. We pollute water, forgetting that water is a carrier of experience and the foundation of life itself. We burn soils, not feeling that soil is a slow but intelligent system. The economy in the human sense is an exchange of advantage. The economy in life's sense is an exchange of sustainability.

What Ecomidas Is at the Level of Idea

If you set aside tokens, platforms, and figures, a simple thought remains: a person should have a direct financial benefit from nature becoming healthier. Not through grants, not through charity, not through fictitious reports, but through a transparent connection: the ecosystem improves → the asset grows in value.

This is not nobility. Not altruism. Not romanticism. It is pure purposefulness. Life as a system uses advantage as a mechanism of learning. If it is advantageous to preserve a forest — the forest will be preserved. If it is advantageous to restore rivers — rivers will be restored. The human being is an adaptive creature. They will go where it is advantageous. The beacons simply need to be repositioned.

Ecomidas Is a Continuation of Life's Global Logic

Life collects experience through forms. Ecosystems collect experience through cycles. The human being collects experience through activity. Ecomidas makes human activity compatible with the activity of nature. It does not "save the world" — it builds a mechanism in which it is advantageous to be intelligent rather than destructive.

Ecomidas is not a project. It is a brick. A brick in the very wall I have called the logic of global reason. Because life leaves ideas in the world just as trees leave seeds. Some will fall on sand. Some on stone. Some on soil. And something will inevitably take root when the right conditions appear.

Conclusion

Consciousness, awareness, reason, intelligence, form of life, experience, purposefulness, Sapiocracy, Ecomidas — all of these are parts of the same line. A line in which man stops considering himself the centre and begins to see himself as a participant. A line where life stops looking like an accident and begins to look like a system that learns from everything it touches.

And if life is truly smarter than us, then perhaps it is leading us precisely there — to the place where man will finally become part of a great process, rather than its mistake.
